§ 05

Four Words for Light.

Lecture 1Photometric Quantities · 25 min

The four quantities you will confuse — and how to keep them straight.

Lighting has the same trap as acoustics: a handful of units that sound interchangeable and aren’t. Get these four right and the rest of Act III is arithmetic.

The chain, source to eye

  • Luminous flux — the lumen (lm). Total visible light a source emits in every direction. A fixture’s “output.” This is the number on the box.
  • Luminous intensity — the candela (cd). Flux in a particular direction (lm per steradian). This is what a luminaire’s photometric distribution actually plots — how the lumens are aimed.
  • Illuminance — the lux (lx) or footcandle (fc). Light arriving on a surface (lm per area). This is what you design to — the target on the workplane, the desk, the stage. 1 fc = 1 lm/ft² ≈ 10.76 lux.
  • Luminance — cd/m² (the “nit”). Light leaving a surface toward the eye — brightness as actually perceived. This is what the audience sees, and the quantity behind glare and contrast.
The one distinction that trips everyone

You design to illuminance; the eye judges luminance.

The lumen method below solves illuminance — how much light lands on a plane. But two surfaces at identical illuminance can look wildly different in luminance depending on their reflectance: a black stage floor and a white page under the same 30 fc are not equally bright to the eye. Hold both in mind — you calculate the first, but you are responsible for the second.

§ 06

The Luminaire.

Lecture 2The Fixture as Data · 25 min

A luminaire is a distribution, a color, and an efficiency — not just a shape.

When you place a fixture in Revit or LightStanza, you are placing a data file. Knowing what that file says is the difference between decorating a ceiling and lighting a room.

The IES file — the luminaire’s “score”

  • An IES (.ies) file is the standardized photometric report for a fixture: candela in every direction, total lumens, input watts. It is what the simulation engine reads — the same role the absorption-coefficient table played in Act I.
  • The photometric distribution is the polar plot of those candela values — the “shape” of the light. Narrow and down for accent; wide and soft for ambient; asymmetric to wash a wall.
  • Lamp lumens vs. fixture (delivered) lumens. The lamp emits X; the housing, lens, and reflector lose some. Always design with delivered lumens — the Φ in the lumen method.
Match Distribution to Task

Four jobs, four shapes.

  • Direct / narrow — downlights over aisles and the stage apron; high efficiency, crisp pools.
  • Diffuse / wide — general house and lobby ambient; even, low-glare coverage.
  • Asymmetric — wall-wash and cove; grazes a surface to reveal material (your Act I diffusers, lit).
  • Accent / adjustable — track and framing projectors; aimed, theatrical, on their own dimmer.
Two Numbers That Decide Quality

Color: CCT and CRI.

CCT (correlated color temperature, in K) sets the mood: ~2700–3000 K warm for the house and lobby; ~3500–4000 K neutral for work and rehearsal.

CRI (color rendering index, 0–100) sets the honesty: aim CRI ≥ 90 wherever skin, costume, or set color must read true — the stage and any display.

Efficacy (lm/W) is the efficiency you’ll defend on energy in Weeks 7–8 — modern LED ~100–150+ lm/W.

The four distributions, drawn

A photometric distribution is a polar plot of candela — the shape the light makes leaving the fixture. These four are the workhorses of a hall. Match the shape to the job; the cut sheets you’ll pull this week each carry one of these plots.

§ 07

The Lumen Method.

This is your Act III analog to the RT60 hand calc: a single zonal-cavity equation that gets you a defensible average maintained illuminance before any simulation. It answers the design question directly — how many fixtures, of what output, to hold a target footcandle level on the workplane?

Average Maintained Illuminance — The Lumen Method
E = ( N × Φ × CU × LLF ) ÷ A
E
average maintained illuminance on the workplane — footcandles (fc) with A in ft², or lux with A in m²
N
number of luminaires
Φ
delivered (fixture) lumens per luminaire
CU
coefficient of utilization — fraction of lumens that reach the workplane (room shape + surface reflectances); typ. 0.4–0.8
LLF
light loss factor — dirt + lamp aging over time; typ. 0.7–0.85
A
workplane (floor) area
Reverse it to size the layout: N = (E × A) ÷ (Φ × CU × LLF). That is the form you’ll use most — target first, fixtures second.

§ 08

Applied Case — A Real Auditorium.

This is not a textbook problem. It is a live Urban Colab renovation — an assembly / banquet auditorium, 4,411 ft², 20-ft ceiling, white walls, rented for weddings and events. The same lumen method you just learned is what we ran on it. It caught a real problem worth real money — and it answers a question you’ll keep asking: does this design actually deliver the light it promises?

The equation is the one on the board, just aggregated — we sum delivered lumens per zone instead of writing N·Φ:

Same Method, Zone Form
E = ( Σlumens × CU × LLF ) ÷ A
Identical to E = (N·Φ·CU·LLF)/A — the fixture count and per-fixture output are rolled into the zone’s total lumens. CU was set per zone from the room cavity ratio; LLF = 0.85 for clean LED.
The finding

The installed fixtures deliver ~7 fc — about a third of target.

The decorative pendants on the current schedule produce roughly 7.2 fc on the main floor. IES RP-2 wants 20–30 fc for assembly and banquet use. The room is running at a quarter-to-a-third of recommended light — somewhere between a cocktail lounge and a dim restaurant, when it’s supposed to host weddings.

What the calc exposed

Delivered lumens are everything.

A 36″ decorative pendant (4,500 lm) was swapped in for the engineer’s 48″ commercial fixture (13,000 lm) — a −65% cut in output. Same ceiling, same room, the fixture looks bigger and brighter on a render. The hand-calc says otherwise.

And CU punishes geometry: the open white-walled floor gets 0.68, but the narrow 8-ft mezzanine bays drop to 0.57 — the same lumens do less work in a tight cavity.

The trade made visible

~11 fc traded for faux alabaster.

The decorative pendants actually win on a couple of specs — CRI 90 vs. 80, and the brass/alabaster look the venue wanted. But they mixed 2700K and 3000K against the engineer’s consistent 3500K, the small one isn’t dimmable, and they gave up roughly 11 fc of light.

None of that is wrong on its face — it’s a trade. The lumen method’s job was to surface it so the owner could decide on purpose, not by accident.

Why you’re seeing this tonight

The lab calc is professional work.

The arithmetic you’ll run in § 11 — E = (N·Φ·CU·LLF)/A, target first, fixtures second — is exactly what flagged a 60% deficit on this project before a single fixture was reordered. A spreadsheet and the IES table did it. When you size your hall tonight, you are doing the same job a consultant gets paid for. Treat your number like it matters, because in practice it does.

§ 09

Lighting the Hall.

Lecture 4Layers, Controls & Energy · 15 min

A performance hall is not one lighting system — it is four, on four controls.

The mistake is to light the room to a single level. Real halls layer independent systems, each with its own target, color, and moment of use.

The four layers

  • House light — warm, dimmable ambient over the audience. Full between acts (~10–15 fc), near-dark during the show. The layer the lumen method sizes.
  • Egress / aisle light — low-level, code-required, stays on through the performance. Step lights and aisle markers, on a protected circuit.
  • Work / rehearsal light — high, neutral, efficient (~50 fc) for set-up and cleaning. Never on during a show.
  • Architectural accent — coves, wall-wash, and feature lighting that reveals your Act I geometry and material. Its own dimmable scene.
The thread into Weeks 7–8 — Electrical

Every layer you add has an energy budget: LPD.

Lighting Power Density (LPD) — installed watts per square foot — is the energy-code limit (ASHRAE 90.1 / IECC) you will have to meet for the building. Tonight, just start tracking the watts behind your fixtures alongside the lumens. In Act III’s electrical weeks you’ll total them into a panel schedule and prove the design is under its LPD allowance. Efficacy (lm/W) is where lumens and watts meet — the more efficient the luminaire, the more light per watt of your budget.

§ 10

Precedent — Lit Halls.

Three reference moves for how performance spaces handle electric light — each hands you something for your own section and switch list.

Layered House Light

Concert-Hall Cove + Cloud

Indirect ambient · warm CCT

Many concert halls hide the house light in coves and uplit clouds — the same suspended geometry that does acoustic work (your Act I reflector cloud) becomes the lighting plane.

Take: let one element earn two systems — reflect sound and bounce light.

Accent & Theatrical

Stage Front-of-House Positions

Aimed accent · CRI ≥ 90

Catwalk and box-boom positions put adjustable, high-CRI accent light on the performer from steep, flattering angles — a separate world from the architectural house light, on its own console.

Take: show lighting is a different system; design the architectural layers and leave hooks for the theatrical rig.

Daylight + Electric

Lobby Dawn-to-Dusk

Dimming · daylight harvesting

The prefunction you just daylit needs an electric layer that blends with it — dimming on photo-sensors so the lobby holds ~25 fc whether the sun is doing the work or the fixtures are.

Take: Act II and Act III meet here — your daylight strategy sets the electric controls.

Efficiency Discipline

The LPD-Lean Box

High efficacy · tight count

Energy-disciplined projects hit their targets with the fewest, most efficient fixtures — high lm/W, good CU from light surfaces, controls that dim when daylight or vacancy allow.

Take: a low fixture count from the lumen method is not a shortcut — it’s the LPD win you’ll defend in Week 8.

§ 11

Lab — First Pass.

Fifty-five minutes to turn tonight’s vocabulary into a number. Pick one space and size its general lighting by hand — this is the seed of your Act III deliverable. Screen-share for live help.

a
Choose & Target
Pick a space, name a target.

Start with your prefunction (it pairs with the Act II daylight work) or the house. Set the maintained target from the IES table and write down the IES category.

  • Record floor area A from your model (ft²).
  • Pick the target E in fc (and note the lux equivalent).
b
Pick a Fixture
Choose a luminaire, read its numbers.

Select a real LED fixture (manufacturer cut sheet or IES file). Record delivered lumens Φ, watts, CCT, CRI, and its distribution type.

  • Use delivered (fixture) lumens, not lamp lumens.
  • Estimate CU (0.4–0.8) and LLF (0.7–0.85) with a one-line justification each.
c
Solve
Run the method, both directions.

Solve N = (E·A)/(Φ·CU·LLF) for fixture count, then plug N back to confirm the achieved E. Use the calculator above to sanity-check.

  • Round N to a buildable grid; report the real achieved E.
  • Note total installed watts (N × W) — your first LPD data point.
d
Log It
Write the decision.

One Decision Log entry: the space, the target, the fixture, your N, and one trade-off (e.g., warmer CCT for the lobby vs. higher CRI for accent).

  • Connect it back: does the layer reveal an Act I/II surface?
  • Flag what you’ll test in LightStanza’s electric module next week.
