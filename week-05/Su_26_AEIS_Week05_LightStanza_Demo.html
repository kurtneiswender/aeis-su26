§ 00  ·  The Idea

Two front-ends, one engine.

It does not matter whether your hall lives in Rhino or in Revit. LightStanza is the destination either way — a browser-based Radiance engine that wants clean geometry, sensible materials, and a measurement grid. The only thing that differs between the two of you is how the model gets in. Once it is in, the steps are identical.

§ 01  ·  Track A
Track A

Rhino → LightStanza

via Collada .dae

Rhino has no live sync — you export a file and drop it in. The whole trick is exporting Collada (not OBJ) so your layers survive, and tagging the surface you want measured before you export.

  1. Check your units.

    DocumentProperties → Units. Feet or meters both work — LightStanza reads the embedded units and converts. Just make sure the model isn’t accidentally in millimeters or inches at building scale.

  2. Tag the measurement surface.

    Draw a flat horizontal surface across the prefunction floor at workplane height and assign it the material name LSGrid. On import LightStanza detects that name and auto-builds your illuminance grid right there — no manual grid drawing later.

  3. Export Collada.

    Select your geometry, then File → Export Selected → Collada (.dae). In the options, check Export layers. Do not use OBJ — OBJ drops the layer names LightStanza relies on to assign materials.

  4. Drop it into LightStanza.

    Drag the .dae straight into the 3D viewer (or the import icon, top toolbar). Processing takes ~30 sec–2 min. When it lands, your Rhino layers show up in the Layers panel on the left.

  5. Eyeball it.

    Orbit once. Confirm the building reads correctly against north, glazing came in as its own surfaces, nothing is missing or floating, and the LSGrid plane is sitting over the prefunction. Wrong? Fix in Rhino and re-export — cheaper than debugging in the browser.

§ 02  ·  Track B
Track B

Revit → LightStanza

via the plugin

Revit syncs directly — no export file. The plugin reads your rooms, glazing, and material assignments and pushes them to the cloud. The prep work is making sure the model is clean inside Revit first.

  1. Install the plugin (once).

    Download from lightstanza.com/plugins. Supports Revit 2021–2026 (not Revit LT). Run the .msi, restart Revit, and it appears under the Add-Ins tab. If you did this for Week 4 setup, you’re ready.

  2. Prep the model.

    Make sure your prefunction Rooms are placed (the plugin uses Rooms to understand spaces) and your glazing is modeled as actual windows / curtain wall, not painted-on faces. Sloppy rooms = sloppy analysis zones.

  3. Launch & sync.

    From the Add-Ins tab, open the LightStanza panel, select the analysis area / rooms, and sync. The model uploads directly to your LightStanza project — skip every Collada step from Track A.

  4. Let materials map across.

    Revit material assignments map automatically to LightStanza materials, and the plugin can place the illuminance grid for you. You still review them — see § 03.

  5. Eyeball it.

    Same check as Rhino: orientation to north, glazing distinct, prefunction grid present. Wrong? Fix in Revit and re-sync.

Take the Rhino path if…

  • Your hall geometry already lives in Rhino
  • You want tight control over exactly which surfaces export
  • You’re comfortable re-exporting to iterate

Take the Revit path if…

  • Your model is a Revit project (most of you)
  • You have Rooms placed and want them as analysis zones
  • You’d rather sync than manage export files
§ 03  ·  Where the paths merge

Once it’s in LightStanza.

From here, Rhino people and Revit people are doing the exact same thing. Four moves, then you run.

  1. Confirm north.

    The sun path is only right if the building rotation is right. Set or verify the project north / location so it matches Detroit (42.33°N). A model rotated 90° will give you confidently wrong answers.

  2. Set materials & reflectances.

    Open the Materials panel (paint-bucket icon). Realistic interior reflectances do real work — light surfaces bounce daylight deeper and cut contrast:

    • Ceiling ≈ 80%  ·  Walls ≈ 50%  ·  Floor ≈ 20%
    • Glazing: set the visible transmittance of your spec’d glass (don’t leave it at a default 90% clear)
  3. Place the grid on the prefunction.

    Open Illuminance Grids. If your LSGrid surface (Rhino) or plugin grid (Revit) is already there, confirm it. Otherwise Add Grid at workplane height ~30″ (0.76 m), 2′ spacing for LEED, orange face up. Cover the prefunction floor only — not the hall, not back-of-house.

  4. Run the annual daylighting analysis.

    Choose the climate-based annual daylight study (the daylighting module, not the electric-lighting one — that waits for Act III). LightStanza returns the two numbers that close Act II:

MetricWhat it answersLEED v4 target
sDAIs the prefunction usefully daylit? (≥300 lux, 50% of hours)≥ 55%
ASEIs it over-lit to the point of glare? (>1000 lux, 250+ hrs)≤ 10%

Export both maps as PNG for your package. A passing sDA with a failing ASE is the classic trap — bright, but you can’t stand to be in it. That’s where tonight’s shading work earns its keep.

Going deeper? This is the live-demo short form. The full step-by-step — materials in detail, blinds & shading, reports, and the electric-lighting module for Act III — is in the Week 4 LightStanza Tutorial.

The point of running it twice isn’t the software — it’s that your daylight numbers don’t care which app you model in. They care whether the geometry, north, and materials are honest.
