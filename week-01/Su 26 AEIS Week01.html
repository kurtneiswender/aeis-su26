Micro-Lecture A — The Book.

Sound, Hearing & Acoustical Comfort 30 min · MEEB Ch. 7

What sound is, before it is calculated.

A vocabulary primer. We will run numbers next week; tonight we earn the words.

1 · Sound as a physical phenomenon (8 min)

  • Frequency & wavelength. 20 Hz → 20 kHz audible range; speech band ~250–4000 Hz; bass below 250 Hz; music up to 8 kHz and beyond.
  • Amplitude & pressure. Sound power vs. sound pressure; the dB scale is logarithmic; doubling distance loses 6 dB in free field.
  • Wavelength & building scale. A 100 Hz wave is ~11 ft long — that is room-sized. High frequencies bounce off small features; low frequencies pass through them.

2 · Hearing as a designed receiver (8 min)

  • Equal-loudness curves — the ear is less sensitive at low and very high frequencies; loudness is not flat.
  • Speech intelligibility. Consonants live in the 2–4 kHz band; lose those and meaning goes with them.
  • Aging & the back row. High-frequency loss with age means design for the worst-case listener, not the test subject.

3 · Acoustical comfort & NC criteria (8 min)

  • NC curves — the standard way to specify a room’s background-noise ceiling.
  • Targets: auditorium NC 20–25; recording studio NC 15; library NC 30; lobby NC 35–40. Each step is audible.
  • Why this matters for our project. Tonight’s plan diagram already implies an NC target — loading dock adjacency, HVAC location, prefunction door swings.

4 · Live demonstrations (6 min)

  • Tone sweep: 50 Hz → 8 kHz, listening for what your headphones reproduce vs. lose.
  • Speech in NC 20 vs. NC 40 noise floor — same talker, two different rooms.
  • Brief Q&A; cue students to listen for these terms in next week’s reading.
§ 04

Chapter 7 — Synopsis.

A distilled reading of MEEB Chapter 7 (Grondzik & Kwok). Use this as the night-before refresher or in-class reference. Diagrams illustrate the relationships students need to see, not just hear about.

7.1Sound, Vibration, Noise — three names for one thing.

All three are pressure variations in an elastic medium. The difference lies in how the receiver experiences them.

  • Sound is audible pressure variation — energy a human can hear.
  • Vibration is pressure variation felt but not heard — tactilely sensed; treated as a distinct subset of acoustics.
  • Noise is unwanted sound — a perceptual judgment, not a measurable physical quantity. Analogous to glare. One person’s music is another’s noise.

For airborne sound, the medium is air; for structure-borne, it is concrete, steel, wood, glass, or their combinations. Sound moves through buildings — not just through their rooms.

7.1 a·b·cSpeed, Wavelength & Frequency.

Three numbers, tied by one equation: λ = c ÷ f.

  • Speed (c): 1130 fps (344 m/s) in air at sea level — effectively constant for design. Sound moves much faster through solids: wood ~10,800 fps, concrete ~12,100 fps, steel ~16,000 fps. This is why structural transmission matters — the building is a faster sound path than the air.
  • Wavelength (λ): the distance one cycle travels. Audible wavelengths span 0.5 in. (20 kHz) to 50 ft (20 Hz). Architectural surfaces respond differently across this range — a 6-in. baffle does nothing to a 30-ft bass wavelength but absorbs high frequencies easily.
  • Frequency (f): cycles per second, in hertz (Hz). Audible 20–20,000 Hz. Speech fundamentals 100–600 Hz with overtones to ~7,500 Hz; critical speech-intelligibility band 300–4,000 Hz.

7.1 dOctave Bands — the design vocabulary.

With 19,980 discrete audible frequencies, designers compress the spectrum into octave bands centered on internationally agreed values: 63 · 125 · 250 · 500 · 1000 · 2000 · 4000 · 8000 Hz. Each band is double the one below. Acoustic specifications, absorption coefficients (NRC), STC ratings, and noise criteria are all expressed in these bands rather than individual frequencies.

Rule of thumb: if a material spec lists eight numbers, those are octave-band coefficients from 63 to 8 kHz.

7.2Hearing — the receiver is not flat.

The ear is a sound-gathering funnel that ends in a mechanical-to-fluid-to-electrical signal chain. Three relevant facts for building design:

  • Presbycusis — high-frequency loss with age, more pronounced in men. By age 60, the threshold at 4,000 Hz has shifted by ~30 dB for men, ~20 dB for women. Design for the worst-case listener, not the test subject.
  • Equal-loudness contours (Fletcher–Munson). The ear is most sensitive between 3–4 kHz — precisely the band that carries consonants. It loses sensitivity sharply below 250 Hz and above 10 kHz. Loudness perception is nonlinear in frequency and in level.
  • Masking, directivity, discrimination. The ear can pick a single instrument from an orchestra (the cocktail-party effect) or be defeated by a steady fan; high frequencies localize, low frequencies do not.

7.4Magnitude — power, pressure, intensity, & the decibel.

Sound has three absolute magnitudes (power, pressure, intensity) and three corresponding levels — logarithmic ratios expressed in decibels (dB). The decibel scale exists because the physical range of audible sound is enormous — 1013:1 — and the ear responds logarithmically.

  • Sound power (W) is the source’s output, independent of room. A jet: 100,000 W. A symphony orchestra: 10 W. Normal speech: 0.000010 W.
  • Sound pressure (Pa) is what the receiver experiences at a given location.
  • Sound intensity (W/cm²) is the energy density at a point.
  • Sound Pressure Level (SPL) = 20 log(p/p₀), with p₀ = 20 µPa (threshold of hearing). The reference is fixed; the level is dimensionless.

Three working rules for designers: +3 dB is barely perceptible. +10 dB sounds twice as loud. +20 dB sounds four times as loud. Decibels add logarithmically — two 60-dB sources do not make 120 dB, they make 63 dB.

7.4 (c)The Inverse Square Law.

A point source in free field radiates equally in all directions. The same energy crossing a sphere of radius r spreads over surface area 4πr² — so intensity falls with the square of distance.

  • Every doubling of distance from a point source loses 6 dB. 80 dB at 5 ft becomes 74 dB at 10 ft, 68 dB at 20 ft.
  • Line sources (a long road, a row of speakers) lose only 3 dB per doubling — they spread cylindrically, not spherically.
  • This holds only in free field. Inside an enclosed space, reflected energy fills the room and the rule breaks down past the “critical distance.”

7.5Noise — annoyance, NC, and RC.

Noise is judged, not measured. Annoyance research (per MEEB) finds annoyance is greater for:

  • louder sound · high-frequency over low · intermittent over continuous
  • pure tones over broadband · moving or unlocatable sources · information-bearing sound (a phone call) over nonsense noise

For specification, Noise Criteria (NC) curves (Beranek) give a single-number ceiling that describes a room’s acceptable continuous background noise across octave bands. A space rated NC-30 means no octave-band level may exceed the NC-30 contour. The NC rating typically falls 5–10 dB below the dBA reading of the same sound.

  • Concert / recital hall · NC 15–20
  • Auditorium / theater · NC 20–25
  • Classroom / conference room · NC 25–30
  • Library / private office · NC 30–35
  • Lobby / open office · NC 35–40
  • Restaurant / retail · NC 40–45

Room Criteria (RC) curves are ASHRAE’s revision of NC, with straight −5 dB/octave slope and regions flagging vibration risk at very low frequencies. RC Mark II is the contemporary update. NC remains the most commonly cited spec in architectural drawings.

7.4 gdBA, dBB, dBC — weighting the meter to the ear.

A sound-level meter reads pressure with equal weight at every frequency — but the ear does not. Three filter networks were standardized to better mimic human response:

  • A-weighting (dBA) — derived from the 40-phon contour; rolls off low frequencies sharply. The universal default for environmental and code work today.
  • B-weighting (dBB) — 70-phon contour; rarely used now.
  • C-weighting (dBC) — nearly flat; used for peak and high-level measurements.

Always identify the weighting alongside the level: “50 dBA” is not the same number as “50 dBC” for the same source.

7.6 · 7.7Vibration & Acoustical Comfort.

Vibration is sound felt below ~20 Hz — usually unwanted, almost always sourced from mechanical or electrical equipment. Its mitigation strategy is different from noise control (mass and isolation rather than absorption).

MEEB closes the chapter with a working definition for an Owner’s Project Requirements (OPR) on acoustics. This is the most useful single page in the chapter for a design course. Typical acoustic design intents:

  • Adequate loudness
  • Appropriate reverberation
  • Acceptable articulation (speech intelligibility)
  • Spatial flexibility
  • Acceptable background sound levels (the NC target)
  • Avoidance of anomalies: echoes, creep, focusing, distortion
  • Cultural appropriateness (a concert hall is not a worship space is not a lecture hall)

BridgeWhat this means for your hall.

For Week 1, the chapter gives you the vocabulary you need to make your first design move — the choice of seating geometry — defensibly. By next week we will add Sabine, Eyring, RT60, and the math of room shaping (Ch. 22). Tonight:

Micro-Lecture B — The Halls.

Precedent Tour — Four Listening Rooms 30 min · supporting material

The four geometries, as built.

Each is a real instance of the diagrams you are about to choose between. We look at plan, section, and what acousticians say about why it works.

Symphony Hall, Boston
1900 · McKim, Mead & White + Wallace Sabine · Shoebox

The reference room for the modern concert hall. Tall narrow rectangular plan; coffered ceiling and side balconies generate strong lateral reflections; statues in niches act as deliberate scatterers. Wallace Sabine tuned the room using his own equation — the first scientifically designed concert hall.

RT60 ~1.85 s · 2,625 seats · W ~75′
Wikipedia BSO Official Wallace Sabine
Philharmonie, Berlin
1963 · Hans Scharoun + Lothar Cremer · Vineyard

Orchestra in the round; terraced seating banks wrap the stage as “vineyards.” Balcony fronts are tuned reflectors. The audience itself becomes part of the room’s acoustic envelope. Acoustic consultant Lothar Cremer shaped each terrace angle specifically for lateral reflection.

RT60 ~1.95 s · 2,440 seats · ~360°
Wikipedia Official Site Hans Scharoun
Festspielhaus, Bayreuth
1876 · Otto Brückwald for Richard Wagner · Fan

Steep fan plan derived from a singer’s problem: how to balance an orchestra of 100 against unamplified voice. Orchestra pit hidden under the stage lip; wood-shell envelope wraps the house. Every sightline aimed at the stage. The room is the argument.

RT60 ~1.55 s · 1,925 seats · covered pit
Wikipedia Official Festival Wagner Brief
Walt Disney Concert Hall, Los Angeles
2003 · Frank Gehry + Yasuhisa Toyota · Vineyard / Sail

Vineyard plan inside an asymmetric sculptural envelope. Douglas-fir convex-curved ceiling sails are individually aimed to redirect energy into the seating. The room was iteratively adjusted in a digital acoustic model before construction. The canonical example of acoustician-architect integration.

RT60 ~2.00 s · 2,265 seats · sail ceiling
Wikipedia LA Phil Official Yasuhisa Toyota

The four plan types — schematic.

Each is a top-down schematic. Stage is amber. Seating is the dark field. Arrows show where early reflections go — or fail to go. Choose which problem you want to solve.

Shoebox

Parallel side walls deliver strong bilateral early reflections. Classic concert-hall geometry. Risk: flutter echo if walls are too smooth.

Fan

Excellent sightlines; all seats face the source. Angled side walls deflect laterals away. Curved back wall can focus sound — must be broken up.

Vineyard

Audience surrounds the stage; balcony fronts are tuned reflectors. High intimacy. Demands more complex BOH and circulation routing.

Horseshoe

Stacked tiers wrap a deep stage. High social intimacy; traditional opera format. Acoustic compromise in side boxes. Tall, complex BOH.

§ 07

Rhino Layer Setup.

Before you start modeling, set up your layers correctly. The Python RT60 script you will run in Week 2 reads layer names directly — if your layers are not named with the AEIS_ prefix, the script skips them silently and your calculations will be wrong.

The convention is simple: every room surface lives on a layer named AEIS_ followed by one of the material keys below. Your sound source lives on AEIS_SOURCE. That is the entire system.

Naming Convention
AEIS_MATERIAL_KEY
Examples:
AEIS_CONCRETE_BARE — bare concrete wall
AEIS_SEATS_UPHOLSTERED — seating block
AEIS_CEILING_CLOUD — reflector panel
AEIS_SOURCE — place one Point object here
Minimum Week 1 Setup
  • Floor surface → AEIS_WOOD_FLOOR or AEIS_CONCRETE_FLOOR
  • Side walls → AEIS_CONCRETE_BARE or AEIS_PLASTER_HARD
  • Ceiling → AEIS_GYPSUM_BOARD or AEIS_CEILING_CLOUD
  • Seating area → AEIS_SEATS_UPHOLSTERED
  • Sound source → one Point on AEIS_SOURCE, stage center ~5 ft above floor

Lab — Volume Studio.

Four breakout rooms, one per geometry. Three to four students per room. The geometry is the group’s conversation; the chosen scheme is the student’s commitment.

Per-group tasks (90 min collaborative)
  • Identify two acoustic strengths and two acoustic concerns of your geometry, in your own words.
  • Sketch one shared section over the assigned plan — ceiling height, slope, balcony if any.
  • Name two design moves that leverage the geometry (e.g., splay angles, balcony fronts) and two moves that compensate for its weaknesses.
Per-student tasks (50 min individual, back in main room or staying in breakout)
  • Choose your starting geometry — it does not have to be the one your breakout discussed.
  • Open the Rhino starter file; lay in the seating block, stage, and a section line.
  • Begin Decision Log entry #01 documenting why you chose what you chose.
