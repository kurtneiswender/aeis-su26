§ 01

What is RT60?

RT60 is the time it takes for a sound to decay by 60 decibels after the source stops. In practice: clap once inside a room, then measure how long you hear it fade to silence. A tile bathroom echoes for a second or two. A cathedral echoes for six. A recording studio is dead in under a third of a second.

RT60 is not just a number on a worksheet — it is the difference between a hall where Beethoven soars and a hall where Beethoven blurs.

The ideal RT60 depends entirely on what the room is for. Speech needs clarity — short decay so consonants don't smear. Music needs richness — longer decay so the room "sings" with the ensemble. There is no universally correct RT60, only appropriate ones.

🔈

Too dry (short RT60)

Sound dies instantly. Great for speech intelligibility, recording studios, TV studios. Terrible for orchestras — musicians can't hear each other, the room feels lifeless.

🏛️

Too live (long RT60)

Sound lingers too long. Successive notes blur together. A choir piece designed for a cathedral (RT60 ≈ 4 s) becomes an unintelligible wash in an overreverberant hall.

The goal of acoustic design is to land your RT60 in the target range for your program — then keep it consistent across all octave bands. A room that is well-tuned at 500 Hz but boomy at 125 Hz still fails.

§ 03

The Sabine Equation

Wallace Clement Sabine derived this formula empirically in 1900 at Harvard by measuring reverberation in different rooms with different amounts of seat cushions. It remains the standard hand-calculation method.

RT60 = 0.161 × V / A
RT60
Reverberation time in seconds — what we are solving for
0.161
Physical constant (SI units). Derived from air density and speed of sound at 20°C
V
Room volume in cubic meters (m³)
A
Total absorption in sabins (m²) = Σ (surface area × absorption coefficient α)

The logic is intuitive: more volume = longer decay (more energy to absorb), and more absorption = shorter decay (surfaces kill energy faster). Doubling the volume doubles RT60. Doubling the total absorption halves it.

What is a sabin?

One sabin (m²) is the absorption equivalent of one square meter of open window — a perfectly absorbing surface. A 100 m² concrete wall with α = 0.02 contributes only 2 sabins. A 100 m² carpet floor with α = 0.57 contributes 57 sabins. The carpet is 28× more effective per unit area.

Worked example: 40 m × 25 m × 12 m room (V = 12,000 m³). Floor is carpet (1,000 m², α = 0.57 → 570 sabins), rear wall is acoustic tile (480 m², α = 0.78 → 374 sabins), everything else is concrete or gypsum (≈ 100 sabins total). Total A ≈ 1,044 sabins.

RT60 = 0.161 × 12,000 / 1,044 = 1.85 s — in range for opera.
§ 04

Absorption Coefficient & NRC

The absorption coefficient (α) is a number from 0.0 to 1.0 that tells you what fraction of sound energy a surface absorbs. α = 0.0 is a perfect mirror — all energy reflected. α = 1.0 is an open window — all energy lost.

Absorption coefficients vary by frequency. Soft materials absorb high frequencies well but often let bass pass through. Hard surfaces reflect almost everything. This is why you need to run Sabine at multiple octave bands — the room may be tuned at 500 Hz but bass-heavy or treble-dead.

§ 05

Eyring vs. Sabine — When Each Applies

Sabine's formula assumes sound energy is uniformly distributed and that absorption is small. When surfaces are highly absorbent (mean α > 0.2), Sabine overestimates RT60 because it underestimates how aggressively each reflection is killed.

The Eyring–Norris equation corrects for this by using the natural log of the mean absorption:

Sabine

RT60 = 0.161 × V / A
  • Use for preliminary estimates
  • Your worksheet hand-calculation
  • Accurate when mean α < 0.2
  • Always overestimates at high α

Eyring

RT60 = 0.161 × V / (−S × ln(1−ᾱ))
  • More accurate when mean α > 0.2
  • What the script uses for status flags
  • Closer to Pachyderm simulation output
  • Use this for design decisions

In a well-designed auditorium with carpet seating and acoustic treatment, mean α at 500 Hz is typically 0.25–0.35 — firmly in Eyring territory. The gap between the two equations is your calibration signal: a large gap means your room has highly non-uniform absorption distribution.

Rubric note: Criterion 2 requires you to submit both Sabine (hand-calc) and Pachyderm (simulation). The script gives you both Sabine and Eyring. Use Sabine as your worksheet value; use Eyring and Pachyderm to validate it.

§ 07

Rhino Workflow

The script reads your Rhino model geometry directly — no export, no plugin license needed. Each room surface must be on a named layer so the script knows what material it is.

Step 1 — Build your room as surfaces

Model each major surface of the room as a separate flat surface or simple brep. Explode any joined polysurfaces (Explode in Rhino). Each surface becomes one entry in your material schedule.

Step 2 — Name your layers

Every surface layer must follow the naming convention AEIS_<MATERIAL>. The script looks up the material key in its table. Underscores only — no hyphens, no spaces.

Step 3 — Run the script

  1. Open the script
    In Rhino: type _RunPythonScript → navigate to aeis_rt60.py → Open.
  2. Select program type
    A dialog appears: choose music_opera for the ARC 5443 auditorium.
  3. Read the command line output
    Scroll up to see the full octave-band table. Look for the STATUS column — you want OK at 500 Hz, 1k Hz, and 2k Hz.
  4. Read the popup
    The summary dialog shows Eyring RT60 (primary) and Sabine (worksheet reference) at 500 Hz, plus an IN RANGE / OUT OF RANGE flag against your target.
  5. Save your report
    Click Yes when asked to save. Choose a file path. The script captures your viewport and generates an HTML report you can open in any browser and print to PDF.

Step 4 — Iterate

If your result is TOO LIVE: swap a large surface to a more absorptive material (or increase area of absorptive surfaces). If TOO DRY: replace acoustic tile or carpet with harder materials, or add volume. Re-run the script immediately — each iteration takes under 30 seconds.

§ 08

Reading the Ray Trace

After computing RT60, the script casts 200 rays from your source point and draws each bounce as a colored line on the AEIS_RAYS layer. The color encodes energy remaining:

Red

High energy — first 1–2 bounces. Direct sound and early reflections.

Orange

Medium energy — 3–4 bounces. Still contributing to room warmth.

Teal

Low energy — late reflections. RT60 is extending but not supporting intelligibility.

Blue

Near-dead — below 5% of original energy. Absorbed or escaped.

Look for gaps (areas with no ray coverage) — those are acoustic shadow zones. Look for dense clusters of late-bounce rays in corners — potential flutter echo or bass buildup. These observations feed directly into your Decision Log entries.

§ 09

What Goes in Your Act I Submission

The RT60 calculation is Criterion 2 of the Act I rubric (4 points). To reach Exemplary:

  • Hand calculation (Sabine): Show the equation with your room's numbers at 500 Hz, 1k Hz, and 2k Hz minimum. Include a material schedule with areas and α values cited from MEEB or the script's source table.
  • Pachyderm simulation: Screenshot the Pachyderm result at 500 Hz. Compare to your Sabine number — if they differ by more than ±0.2 s, explain why.
  • RT60 target justification: State your program type and cite the target range. Don't just say "opera" — say "mixed speech/music program: target 1.6–2.0 s per MEEB Table 27.3."
  • Dominant absorber: Name the surface that contributes the most sabins. (Usually carpet floor or upholstered seats in a 500-seat house.)
  • Decision Log entry: At least one entry connecting a material swap to a specific RT60 change. Reference actual numbers.
