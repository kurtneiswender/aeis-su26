§ 05

Solar Geometry & the Sun Path.

Lecture A — Solar Geometry MEEB Ch. 10 · pp. 1–15

The sun is a moving source — design for all of it.

Before you put a window anywhere, you need to know exactly where the sun is at 9 AM on December 21 and 3 PM on June 21. Those two moments bracket your entire range of exposure.

1 · Altitude & Azimuth — The Two Angles You Will Use Every Day

  • Altitude is the sun’s angle above the horizon, measured in degrees from 0° (horizon) to 90° (directly overhead). In Detroit (42.3°N): summer noon altitude ≈ 71°, winter noon altitude ≈ 25°. The low winter sun is the critical case for glare.
  • Azimuth is the compass bearing of the sun measured from north. East = 90°, South = 180°, West = 270°. A south-facing window receives sun all day in winter; an east-facing window is done by noon.
  • Together they give you a vector in 3D space. If you know altitude and azimuth for any hour and date, you know exactly which surfaces are sunlit and where shadows fall.
  • The sun path diagram — the stereographic sun chart for your latitude — overlays all hours and dates simultaneously. One chart tells you everything: when each compass face receives direct sun, for how many hours per season, and at what altitude (shading depth).

2 · How Latitude Changes Everything

  • At 42°N (Detroit, Chicago, Boston), the sun stays well south of zenith all year. The northern sky is always indirect diffuse light — no direct sun. A north aperture never produces glare from direct sun and is the most consistent light source architecturally.
  • South apertures are the most controllable: fixed horizontal overhangs can block the high summer sun (altitude ~71°) while admitting the low winter sun (altitude ~25°). The shading overhang depth is predictable geometry.
  • East and west apertures are the most difficult: low morning and afternoon sun enters at near-horizontal angles that no fixed horizontal overhang can block. Vertical fins or deep setbacks are required.
  • This is not just theory — it drives your first aperture decision tonight: which face of your hall has the least daylight conflict with the acoustic geometry?

3 · Equinox vs. Solstice — The Two Reference Conditions

  • LightStanza, Ladybug, and every daylight tool will ask you to run at least two solar positions: typically June 21 (summer solstice, highest sun, longest day) and December 21 (winter solstice, lowest sun, shortest day). Some tools also include March/September 21 (equinox, mid-range conditions).
  • Summer solstice is the glare risk case: sun enters at high altitude through south apertures and at low altitude through east/west apertures in the morning and afternoon. This is when overheating and occupant discomfort peak.
  • Winter solstice is the daylight adequacy case: if your prefunction reads well in December at noon, it reads well all year. If it fails in December, you need more aperture.
  • Run both tonight. They tell a complete story together that neither alone can tell.

4 · IESNA’s Six Essential Ingredients for Effective Daylighting

  • Plan the interior for daylight access. Room depth, ceiling height, and partition layout determine whether daylight reaches work surfaces. Deep floor plates without atria or light wells are inherently dark.
  • Minimize direct sunlight near critical visual tasks. A sunlit patch on a work surface creates a luminance ratio that makes everything around it appear dim. In a prefunction, direct sun on a bar-top or registration desk is a glare problem, not an amenity.
  • Design spaces to minimize glare. Glare is a luminance contrast problem, not an illuminance problem. A 50,000-cd/m² window next to a 200-cd/m² wall is uncomfortable regardless of how much total light is in the room. Reveals, splayed jambs, and adjacent light walls all reduce contrast.
  • Zone electric lighting for daylight-responsive control. Daylight zones should have their own circuit, separate from the rest of the space. A prefunction with one lighting zone defeats the purpose of the daylight design — the whole space stays on at full output even when half of it is flooded with noon sun.
  • Provide daylight-responsive controls. Photosensors that dim or switch electric lighting as daylight increases are required to realize the energy savings that justify the glass cost. LEED EQ credits assume controls are in place.
  • Commission and maintain the system. Controls drift. Sensors get dust. Blinds get drawn and never opened. A daylighting strategy is only as good as its ongoing commissioning. This is the ingredient most often skipped in practice.

5 · Human Factors — Why Daylight Is Not Optional

  • Circadian rhythms. The human body uses light intensity and spectrum (blue-rich morning light, warm afternoon light) to regulate sleep/wake cycles, cortisol levels, and alertness. Occupants in daylit spaces show measurably better alertness and reduced fatigue. This is a physiological need, not an aesthetic preference.
  • Productivity and learning outcomes. Research in daylit schools shows students performing measurably better on standardized math and reading tests in classrooms with high daylighting than matched classrooms with only electric light. The effect is strongest in the first three hours of the school day — when blue-rich morning light is highest.
  • Glare as the failure mode. The same daylight that improves alertness becomes a liability the moment it causes glare. Occupants draw blinds and never open them again. The three glare management strategies in order of preference: exterior fixed shades (most effective, never interfere with views), diffusing systems (fritted glass, light shelves, prismatic film), and occupant-controlled adjustable shades (least effective, but necessary fallback for east/west exposures).

6 · Site Strategy — Orientation, Zoning, Building Form

  • Elongate the building on the east-west axis. A building with its long axis running east-west maximizes the south facade (controllable with horizontal shading) and minimizes the east and west facades (difficult to shade). For a hall that must be north-south on stage axis, the site placement of the prefunction lobby becomes the critical move.
  • Zone by function, schedule, and orientation. Group spaces with similar daylight needs together. In a performing arts center: back-of-house support spaces can be on the north or east; the prefunction should face south or southeast; the hall itself is internally determined by acoustics and sight lines.
  • Building form and daylight. Thick buildings have dark cores; thin buildings achieve complete sidelighting. A 12m-deep floor plate can be fully daylit by sidelighting alone (6m from each side). Beyond 24m depth, supplementary toplighting or atria are required for adequate sDA. The performing arts hall is almost always too thick to daylit from the sides — this is one reason the prefunction is the primary daylighting canvas.
  • Envelope strategy by orientation. South facade: horizontal overhangs sized for summer shading (overhang depth = window height × tan(90° − summer altitude)). North facade: high glazing admits diffuse sky without glare; no shading needed. East/West facades: vertical fins or deep setbacks; avoid large unshaded glass.

7 · Sidelighting Principles — The 15/30 Rule

  • 15-foot fully daylit zone. The area within approximately 15 feet (4.5 m) of a window wall receives adequate diffuse daylight for most task conditions (DF ≥ 2%). This is the “prime real estate” of sidelighting — put primary functions here.
  • 15 to 30 feet: partially daylit. From 15 to 30 feet (4.5–9 m), daylight levels drop sharply and become supplementary. Daylight-responsive electric lighting dims when daylight is present but cannot fully replace it. Transitional functions (corridors, secondary gathering, support) fit here.
  • Beyond 30 feet: electrically lit. Beyond 30 feet (9 m) from a window, daylight is no longer a reliable source for visual tasks. The space requires dedicated electric lighting regardless of exterior conditions. This is where the hall house sits.
  • Window height extends penetration. Raising the window head from 8 ft to 12 ft can push the fully daylit zone from 15 ft to 20+ ft. This is why clerestories and high-set windows outperform equal-area punched windows at mid-height — the light ray angle is shallower and penetrates farther into the room.
  • Bilateral lighting is the most even. Two window walls facing each other eliminate the dark zone that forms opposite a single window wall. Where bilateral lighting is impossible (as in most halls), a light shelf or interior light-redirecting surface on the opposing wall acts as a substitute diffuser.
  • Aperture detailing for contrast control. Splay the window jamb at 45° minimum to transition luminance gradually from window to wall. An unsplayed window is a high-luminance disk surrounded by low-luminance wall — the definition of discomfort glare. Painting the adjacent wall light (reflectance ≥ 60%) further reduces contrast.

The performing arts typology creates a direct conflict: a hall that needs to be dark and a lobby that needs to celebrate daylight. The projects below each handle that tension differently. Study the aperture strategy, not just the imagery.

The strategy: treat the hall as an opaque masonry object and wrap circulation around it in glass. The hall itself has zero windows.

Clerestory · Diffuse North Light

Kimbell Art Museum — Fort Worth, 1972

Louis Kahn’s cycloid vaults admit a narrow north-facing clerestory slot at the crown. A reflective aluminum louver below the slot converts direct sun into diffuse ambient light washing the entire vault. No direct sun ever reaches the floor. What to Notice

In every case, the hall is dark. The prefunction is the daylight move.

These buildings range from a fully opaque hall (Kimbell, Disney) to a transparent one (Lindemann), but the logic is consistent: blackout integrity in the performance volume; daylight celebrated in arrival and circulation. Your Act II assignment follows this same partition. The hall house is excluded from sDA/ASE analysis by design. The prefunction is where you prove daylight quality.

§ 06

The Hall, the Prefunction, & Glass.

Lecture B — Where Glass Can (and Cannot) Go MEEB Ch. 10 · pp. 15–25

A performance hall and its prefunction are two different problems.

The house must be controllably dark. The prefunction must be daylit and inviting. Designing glass as if they were the same space is one of the most common errors in auditorium design.

1 · The Auditorium: A Room That Must Be Dark

  • Unlike an office or classroom, a performance hall is an explicitly blackout-capable environment. The theatrical lighting rig cannot compete with even modest daylight. A single uncontrolled window in the house can render stage lighting ineffective at 2 PM.
  • This does not mean zero glass in the house. It means every aperture must be operable blackout or strategically positioned where direct sun is architecturally impossible — typically clerestories oriented north, or high-level apertures designed around the season/time of use.
  • The performance schedule is your most important design parameter. An evening-only venue has far more daylight freedom than a school auditorium used for afternoon assemblies.
  • Decision Log question for tonight: what times of day does your hall operate, and does that constrain which faces can have glass?

2 · The Prefunction: A Room That Should Celebrate Daylight

  • The prefunction lobby, concourse, and bar areas are your primary daylight canvas. They operate when people are arriving and during intermission — typically in the evening for professional venues, but during the day for academic and community halls.
  • The prefunction has no acoustic constraint equivalent to the hall: it is a sociable, reverberant environment by design. Glass is not only acceptable; it is expected. A prefunction that reads dark and cave-like in daylight fails its social function.
  • For this course, treat the prefunction as the primary sDA/ASE analysis zone. This is where your Daylight Factor and climate-based metrics are measured. The hall gets blackout integrity; the prefunction gets daylight quality.
  • Glazing strategies to explore: floor-to-ceiling curtain wall (high sDA, high ASE risk), clerestory band above an opaque base (lower sDA, excellent glare control), punched windows with deep reveals (moderate both metrics, strong contrast with interior).

3 · Daylight Metrics — What They Actually Mean

  • Daylight Factor (DF): the ratio of interior illuminance to simultaneous exterior illuminance under a uniform overcast sky, expressed as a percentage. Simple, climate-agnostic, good for comparisons. A DF of 2% is considered minimum adequate; 5%+ is well-lit. Limitation: it ignores direct sun and clear sky conditions entirely — it assumes overcast. Still useful as a quick check.
  • sDA (Spatial Daylight Autonomy): the percentage of the floor area that receives at least 300 lux for at least 50% of occupied hours, calculated using a full year of climate data. The LEED v4 and IES LM-83 target is sDA ≥ 55% for “nominally daylit.” This is your primary metric for the prefunction.
  • ASE (Annual Sunlight Exposure): the percentage of the floor area that receives more than 1,000 lux for more than 250 hours per year. ASE measures too much sun — direct beam glare and overheating risk. LEED v4 target: ASE ≤ 10%. If your sDA is high but your ASE is also high, you have a glare problem that your sDA score is masking.
  • Read them together. High sDA + low ASE = well-daylit without glare. High sDA + high ASE = daylit but uncomfortable. Low sDA + low ASE = dark but glare-free. Low sDA + high ASE = worst case: only direct beam spots, not useful diffuse light.
Reading sDA & ASE Together
sDA high · ASE low
Target zone.

Well-daylit without direct glare. Diffuse light dominates. Typical result of north clerestory or south aperture with effective overhang. Aim for this in the prefunction.

sDA high · ASE high
Glare risk.

The space is daylit but direct beam is excessive. Occupants will draw blinds; effective sDA drops. Typical of unshaded south or west glass. Add shading devices or reduce WWR.

sDA low · ASE low
Dark but controlled.

Insufficient daylight but no glare. Typical of north-facing or deeply recessed apertures that are undersized. Increase aperture area or use light-redirecting devices.

sDA low · ASE high
Worst case.

Only direct-beam spots, no useful diffuse light. Deeply unsatisfying visually. Typical of small punched windows with no shading on east or west face. Complete redesign of aperture approach.

Key Question for Tonight’s Lab

Where does glass belong on your specific hall?

Every hall has a different site, orientation, and program. Tonight you find your answer for your hall. Start with the compass: which face of your hall faces north? That face gets the easiest daylight question — it’s always indirect. Then work outward. What faces do your prefunction spaces occupy? What is the performance schedule that constrains house apertures?

The sun path doesn’t change. Your answer to these questions does, based on how you positioned your hall on site.
