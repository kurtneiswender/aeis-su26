  • RT60 is wildly different from hand calc. If volume is correct, compare the material schedule in the HTML report to your hand-calc worksheet. A single large surface with the wrong material assignment dominates the result.

§ 05

The Core Distinction.

Week 2 was about controlling how sound behaves inside a room. Week 3 is about controlling how sound moves between rooms. The tools, metrics, and materials are almost entirely different — and using an absorption solution where an isolation solution is needed is one of the most common errors in acoustic design.

Week 2 — Room Acoustics

Absorption.

Porous, lightweight materials convert sound energy to heat as it passes through their fibers. They reduce the reverberant field within a room — controlling how long sound lingers and how loud the room becomes under sustained input.

Examples: Acoustic tile, fiberglass panels, carpet, fabric, foam. Dense/rigid materials are poor absorbers.

Metric: NRC (Noise Reduction Coefficient) — a number from 0 to 1.0 rating how absorptive a surface finish is. 0 = perfect mirror, 1.0 = perfect sponge. NRC is a material property, rated by surface finish.

  • Works by converting sound energy to heat
  • Controls reverb, RT60, and room buildup
  • Has almost no effect on transmission through walls
Week 3 — Noise Control

Isolation.

Dense, impermeable materials resist the motion induced by a sound wave, converting its energy to tiny vibrations rather than passing it through. Stopping transmission requires mass, airtightness, and decoupling — not porosity.

Examples: Drywall, glass, concrete, CMU, sheet metal. Porous/lightweight materials are poor barriers.

Metrics: TL (Transmission Loss, dB) — intrinsic property of the construction itself. STC (Sound Transmission Class) — single-number summary of TL. NR (Noise Reduction, dB) — the actual level difference measured in a real building, which depends on TL plus room conditions.

  • Works by resisting sound-induced vibration
  • Effective against airborne and structure-borne transmission
  • Destroyed by any air path, crack, or rigid connection
Key Rule — From Your Slides

Good barriers are generally bad absorbers — and vice versa.

A material cannot be both dense/impermeable (good barrier) and porous/lightweight (good absorber) at the same time. Concrete blocks sound well but reflects nearly everything back. Acoustic tile absorbs reflections beautifully but lets sound pass right through it.

The design implication: you will often need both — a massive partition for isolation, lined on the source side with absorptive material to reduce the reverberant build-up that drives energy into the wall. The lining does not replace the partition; it reduces the load the partition must handle.

What Happens at a Boundary — MEEB Fig. 23.1

Every surface splits incident sound into three paths.

When sound strikes any building surface: some is reflected back into the source room; some is absorbed (converted to heat within the material — a very small fraction); and some is transmitted through to the adjacent space. The fractions depend on the material. For a glass partition: mostly reflected, tiny amount absorbed, small but significant amount transmitted. For acoustic tile: mostly absorbed and reflected, almost nothing transmitted. For a thick concrete wall: mostly reflected, nearly nothing transmitted.

The equation is simply: α + ρ + τ = 1, where α is absorption, ρ is reflection, τ is transmission. A good barrier material has τ near zero. A good absorber has α near 1.

The Four Metrics — What Each Measures
Material Property
NRC

Noise Reduction Coefficient — how absorptive a surface finish is. Scale 0–1.0. Controls reverb and RT60 within a room. Used in Week 2 calculations. Has nothing to do with how much sound passes through a wall.

Construction Property (lab tested)
TL

Transmission Loss — how much a partition assembly reduces sound, measured in dB. Tested in a controlled lab; the room conditions are normalized out. An intrinsic property of the wall itself, independent of the spaces on either side.

Building Condition (field measured)
NR

Noise Reduction — the actual dB level difference between two adjacent spaces in a real building. Depends on TL, the area of the shared partition, and the absorption of the receiving room. What occupants actually experience. NR ≈ TL for most planning purposes.

Note: NR and NRC share similar names but measure completely different things and are unrelated.

Single-Number Rating
STC

Sound Transmission Class — a single number derived by fitting a standard contour to the TL values measured across 16 one-third octave bands. Used for code compliance and specification. Higher STC = better isolation. The number you look up in MEEB tables.

§ 06

How Barriers Work.

TL · NR · Mass Law · Decoupling · Leaks MEEB §23.7–23.11

Sound insulation — three ideas and one rule.

Mass stops sound. Decoupling stops the bypass. A single opening destroys both. Structure-borne is a separate path entirely.

1 · TL vs. NR — Why the Distinction Matters

  • TL is a property of the wall. It does not change based on what rooms are on either side. It is what you specify.
  • NR is what you measure in the field — the actual level difference. A highly absorptive receiving room (NC 20 music hall, heavily treated) produces higher NR than a reverberant one from the same wall, because the transmitted sound decays faster. The relationship is: NR = TL − 10 log(S ÷ AR) where S is partition area and AR is the absorption of the receiving room.
  • For a live receiving room, NR ≈ TL − 1 dB. For a dead receiving room, NR can exceed TL by 5–7 dB. The wall is still the primary lever; the room condition is a modifier.

2 · The Mass Law — In Plain Terms

  • Think of a wall as a giant drumskin. A lighter drumskin vibrates more easily — more vibration, more transmission. Doubling the mass of the drumskin requires twice the force to move it, which reduces vibration by about 6 dB.
  • The rule: every doubling of surface mass adds roughly 6 dB of TL. A standard 1/2” GWB wall weighs about 6 lb/ft² and achieves TL ≈ 35 dB. To gain 20 more dB by mass alone, you need to multiply wall weight by 10× — roughly 60 lb/ft², about 5” of solid concrete. For 30 more dB, you need 32× the weight — around 200 lb/ft², or 16” of concrete.
  • The implication: mass alone is practical only up to STC ~45. Beyond that, the structural loads become prohibitive. The solution is decoupling.

3 · Decoupling — Where the Real Gains Come From

  • If two wall leaves are rigidly connected — by studs, by wire ties, by continuous concrete, by a back-to-back outlet — they behave as one heavy leaf. The cavity provides no benefit.
  • Staggered studs: alternating studs on a wider plate so neither leaf shares a framing member. Each leaf vibrates somewhat independently. +9 dB over baseline.
  • Resilient channel: a thin spring-metal hat channel between the stud and the GWB. Allows the drywall to flex without directly coupling to the stud. Effective, but a single rigid screw through the channel destroys most of the benefit.
  • Double stud: two entirely separate stud frames with an air gap. The best stud-based strategy, achieving STC 55+ with proper execution.
  • Cavity fill: adding batt insulation inside the cavity absorbs sound that couples through the air gap. Works synergistically with decoupling; adds about 3–5 dB.

4 · Composite Walls & Leaks — The Weakest Link

  • A wall with only 1% of its area open (a gap under a door, an uninsulated outlet) has a maximum achievable TL of 20 dB — regardless of how massive the solid portion is. The math is unforgiving.
  • A keyhole loses 3 dB. A hairline crack loses 3 dB. An unlined penetration through both leaves of a double wall can drop STC by 10+ points.
  • Design checklist for every high-STC partition: seal perimeter at floor/ceiling with acoustical sealant; offset back-to-back outlets; no duct through the wall (route around or use sound-attenuating flexible connection); doors to be acoustically rated and fully gasketed.

5 · Structure-Borne Sound — A Separate Path

  • Airborne isolation strategies (heavy walls, staggered studs) do nothing to stop vibration that travels through the building structure. A pump on a concrete slab radiates noise several floors away even through a perfectly sealed room.
  • The control strategy: break the rigid path. Mechanical equipment must sit on isolation mounts (springs or neoprene pads) with an inertia slab. Pipes must connect to equipment with flexible couplings. Ductwork must use flexible connectors at the air handler.
  • The design principle from your slides: “Isolate, eliminate contact, use flexible connections at all penetrations.”

§ 09

Precedent — Buffer Zones.

Before the lab, a brief look at how professional acoustic consultants and architects address the isolation challenge at the building-planning scale. The principle: the best wall is the one you don’t have to build. Strategic placement of program spaces creates air gaps and buffer zones that do acoustic work before any partition is specified.

Plan Strategy — Concentric Buffer Ring

The Wrapped Hall

Boston Symphony Hall • McKim, Mead & White, 1900 • STC equivalent ~65+

The shoebox hall is wrapped in a thick ring of secondary program: service corridors, practice rooms, storage, dressing rooms, and lobby on all sides. No exterior wall directly faces the hall. Every noise source is at least two partitions and an occupied buffer space away from the audience.

The mass masonry exterior wall provides baseline isolation. The buffer ring provides distance attenuation. The result: 125 years of use with no significant exterior noise intrusion — and no mechanical room adjacent to the hall.

Plan Strategy — Hall Within Galleria

The Box in a Box

Kimmel Center • Rafael Viñoly Architects, 2001 • Verizon Hall STC ~60

The Verizon Hall concert volume sits as a discrete object inside a glass-roofed galleria. The galleria serves as a large air buffer on all sides — separating the acoustic shell from the HVAC equipment, the street noise, and the secondary hall (Perelman). The two hall volumes share no structure and no mechanical system.

The galleria also provides a transition zone for pre-concert crowd noise before patrons enter the hall, and its thermal mass helps regulate humidity. The acoustic separation was achieved without unusually thick walls by maximizing the air gap.

Section Strategy — Floating Room

The Room Within a Room

Walt Disney Concert Hall • Gehry Partners / Nagata Acoustics, 2003

The 2,265-seat hall is constructed as a structurally independent box inside the building envelope. The hall walls do not touch the building frame; they are supported on vibration isolation mounts. Mechanical equipment is in a basement separated by a full story of structure, connected only via flexible duct and pipe connections.

The floor of the hall is a floating concrete slab on spring isolators. A 6” air gap runs around the perimeter of the hall box. This approach achieves NC 10–15 background noise levels in the hall — quieter than any conventional construction can achieve through partition mass alone.

Design Principle — Buffer Zone Toolkit

What architects control.

Programming • Plan Organization • Section Strategy

The acoustician controls material specifications. The architect controls adjacency. As your slides note: the architect’s primary lever is the path — the distance and barriers between source and receiver.

In plan: surround the hall with non-sensitive program: lobbies, restrooms, coat rooms, storage, circulation. Keep loading docks, mechanical rooms, and parking structures at the opposite end of the building from stage.

In section: locate mechanical rooms above or below the hall with a full unoccupied floor as a buffer. Float the hall slab on isolation mounts. Consider a basement mech floor so no equipment shares a slab with the audience.

For your Act I: annotate your section to show where your mechanical room is relative to the hall. If it is directly adjacent to or below the stage, that is a significant acoustic liability to acknowledge in your Decision Log.

Lab — Adjacency Noise Analysis.

Here is what you are doing in tonight’s lab, step by step. Work in breakout groups of 3–4, but each student completes their own worksheet. Use your group to pressure-test your reasoning — especially the source dB estimates and the assembly selection logic.

A
Adjacency Map
Draw your noise context.

Take your auditorium plan (from your Rhino or Revit model, or a sketch). Draw every space that shares a wall, floor, or ceiling with the hall. Label each with: (1) what the space is, (2) what the primary noise source is, and (3) approximately when that noise occurs (day, night, during performances).

  • Loading dock / service corridor → fork lifts, truck backup alarms, doors slamming: ~85–90 dB
  • Mechanical room → HVAC fans, compressors: ~75–85 dB, structure-borne dominant
  • Lobby / prefunction → crowd noise at intermission: ~70–75 dB
  • Practice rooms → piano, amplified instruments: ~80–90 dB
  • Exterior → traffic, HVAC condensers on roof: ~55–75 dB depending on site
B
Required NR
Calculate what you need.

For your worst-case adjacency (the loudest source relative to your hall’s NC target), calculate the required NR. The formula is simple: Required NR = Source Level (dB) − NC target for the hall. Your NC target should be from your Week 1 analysis.

  • NC target for a music performance hall: 20–25 dB
  • Loading dock example: 87 dB source
  • Required NR: 87 − 22 = 65 dB
  • Use NR ≈ TL for planning → you need a partition with TL / STC ≈ 65
  • That is a high-performance double stud + masonry system
C
Assembly Selection
Specify the partition.

From MEEB Tables 23.1–23.2, the STRACAT tool, or manufacturer data, find an assembly with STC ≥ your required NR. Sketch the cross-section (layers, dimensions). Note: you are specifying this assembly for one specific critical partition in your building — this is real design documentation.

  • Look up STC for your assembly type in MEEB Table 23.1 (stud) or 23.2 (masonry)
  • Try the STRACAT tool to compare alternatives: hudexchange.info/stracat
  • Note the total wall thickness — does it fit your floor plan grid?
  • Check every flanking path: duct through that wall? Door? Back-to-back outlet? Window?
  • Each flanking path identified is a trade-off to document in your Decision Log
D
Decision Log
Document your reasoning.

Write Decision Log entries 3 and 4 before leaving the breakout room. Entry 3 is your NC target decision. Entry 4 is your wall assembly decision for the worst-case adjacency. Both should follow your established DL format: decision, system, space, reasoning, trade-off acknowledged.

  • Entry 3: NC target = [value] for [space type]. Rationale: [program use, MEEB recommendation for type, client priorities].
  • Entry 4: Assembly = [name or description], STC = [value], for the [adjacency] partition. Reasoning: required NR = [X] dB based on [source] at [level]. Trade-off: [wall thickness → floor plan impact / cost / construction sequence].
Debrief Discussion Prompt What is your hall’s most difficult adjacency — not the loudest source, but the one that creates the most architectural constraints? How does the required wall thickness, construction type, or structural load affect your floor plan, your building section, or your design concept?
