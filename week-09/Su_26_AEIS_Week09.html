§ 03

Two Systems We Never Wired.

Look at what you have built. In Act I you gave the hall a shape and tuned it until the reverberation flattered the program. In Act II you cut it open to the sun and then defended it against glare. In Act III you lit it after dark, counted the watts, drew the panel, and put an array on the roof to pay for them.

Three acts, and the hall still cannot do two things a real hall must do. It cannot speak — there is no way to put a voice into that room and have it arrive at the back row intelligible. And it cannot fail safely — cut the utility feed mid-performance and every light you designed goes out at once, in a dark room, with four hundred people in it.

Those two gaps are not oversights in the assignment. They are the two systems the course catalog names and the Acts had no room for: electronic sound reinforcement, and utility connection, emergency backup, and safety. Tonight we close them — not to consultant depth, but far enough that you know what the consultant is asking you for, and far enough to put both into the Encore.

The symmetry Notice what both of tonight’s systems have in common: each one reaches back and taxes something you already designed. The sound system taxes your RT60 — the reverberation that makes the music bloom is the reverberation that smears the speech. The emergency system taxes your panel — the egress layer you drew as one more lighting zone turns out to be a legally separate branch with its own source. Every system in this building is paid for by another one. That is the entire thesis of the Encore.
§ 04

Sound Reinforcement.

Lecture 1Giving the Hall a Voice · 33 min

Your RT60 was designed for music. Now put a microphone in the room.

Room acoustics and sound reinforcement are not the same discipline, and they are frequently at war. The room that makes a cello bloom is the room that turns a spoken sentence into mush — and the sound system is what you buy to fix the room you deliberately built.

Why reinforce at all

  • The unamplified case is the exception, not the rule. A symphony in a 1.8 s hall needs no help. But your hall will also host a lecture, a graduation, a panel discussion, an amplified band, and a fire alarm with a voice-evacuation message. Every one of those needs a system.
  • Reverberation is intelligibility’s enemy. Speech runs at roughly 4–5 syllables per second. If the tail of one syllable is still bouncing around the room when the next one arrives, the consonants — which carry nearly all the meaning — are buried under the vowels. A hall tuned to 1.8 s for music will be hostile to unreinforced speech.
  • The metrics. STI (Speech Transmission Index, 0–1; above 0.60 is good, below 0.45 is poor) and C50 (clarity — early-to-late energy ratio at 50 ms, the speech version of the C80 you may have met for music). You will not compute these by hand. You need to know they exist and what pushes them the wrong way: reverberation and distance.
The One Number That Matters

Critical distance — where the room starts winning.

Stand near a talker in any room and you hear mostly direct sound — it arrives first, it is clean, and it falls off predictably with distance (6 dB per doubling, inverse-square). Walk away and the direct sound keeps dropping, but the reverberant field — the diffuse wash of reflections filling the room — stays roughly constant everywhere.

Somewhere between those two, they cross. That crossing point is the critical distance, Dc. Closer than Dc, you hear the talker. Farther than Dc, you hear the room, and intelligibility falls off a cliff that no amount of amplifier power can climb — because turning it up raises the direct and the reverberant field together. You cannot out-power a reverberant room. You can only out-aim it.

That is the whole intellectual content of loudspeaker design in one sentence: get more direct sound onto the listener without adding energy to the room. Directivity, not wattage.

Run this on your own hall tonight — every input is already sitting in your Act I worksheet. If your Dc for an unaided talker (Q ≈ 2) comes out at 12 feet and your back row is 70 feet from the stage, you have just proven, numerically, that your hall requires a sound system. That single sentence, with your two numbers in it, is the core of the reinforcement page in your Encore.
The three system archetypes

Pick one and defend it. The choice follows from room shape, ceiling height, and reverberation — not from a catalog.

Archetype 01

Central Cluster

One point source, hung over the proscenium

A single cluster of directional loudspeakers above and slightly forward of the stage, aimed to cover the seating and miss the walls and ceiling. Sound arrives from roughly where the performer is, so the ear localizes correctly.

Use when: the hall has height, a defined stage, and one dominant source. The default for a proscenium hall.

Costs you: a structural hang point and a sightline. That cluster is visible, it is heavy, and it needs to be in the section you draw.

Archetype 02

Distributed Ceiling

Many small sources, close to every listener

A grid of ceiling loudspeakers, each covering a small patch of floor. Every listener is inside the critical distance of the nearest speaker, so intelligibility holds even in a reverberant, low room.

Use when: the ceiling is low, the room is long and flat, or there is no single stage — lobbies, prefunction, corridors, back-of-house. This is almost certainly the right answer for your prefunction space.

Costs you: localization. The voice comes from the ceiling, not the person. Fine for paging, wrong for performance.

Archetype 03

Line Array

Vertical column, tight vertical control

A column of drivers whose combined output narrows vertically — it throws far and level, and can be curved to place energy on the seats and nowhere else. The long-throw solution for a big, reverberant volume.

Use when: the hall is deep, the RT60 is high, and you need to hit row 30 without exciting the room.

Costs you: size, weight, and money. The most visible option in the room.

Not Optional

Assistive Listening

ADA / IBC requirement — assembly occupancy

An assembly space with amplified sound must provide an assistive listening system: a hearing loop (induction loop, works directly with a hearing aid’s T-coil — the best experience by far), or an IR / RF receiver system.

This is a code requirement, not a courtesy. Receiver counts scale with seat count. A hearing loop is a wire buried in your floor — which means you have to coordinate it, in the floor assembly you already detailed for acoustics.

Precedence / Haas Effect

Delay the loudspeaker so the audience hears the person.

If two copies of the same sound reach your ear within about 5–35 milliseconds, you do not hear an echo. You hear one sound, and you localize it to whichever copy arrived first — even if the second one is up to ~10 dB louder. That is the precedence (Haas) effect, and it is the single most elegant trick in audio.

The application: sound travels at roughly 1,130 ft/s, while the signal in the wire is effectively instantaneous. So the loudspeaker beats the performer's own voice to the back of the room, and the audience localizes the sound to the ceiling — an unnerving, disembodied effect. The fix is to delay the loudspeaker electronically so the performer’s natural sound arrives first, and the reinforcement follows 10–20 ms behind. The room is louder; the voice still comes from the human being.

Rule of thumb: delay (ms) ≈ (distance from source to speaker, in feet) ÷ 1.13, then add ~10–15 ms of “Haas cushion.” Every DSP does this in software. You do not have to compute it — you have to know it is why the system needs a DSP at all.

The signal chain — and where it hits your drawings

Microphone → mixer → DSP (equalization, delay, limiting) → amplifiers → loudspeakers. As an architect you will never spec the DSP. You will be held responsible for the four places that chain lands in your building:

  • The rack room. Amplifiers and DSP live in an equipment rack. That rack is 6–10 ft² of floor you have not drawn, it dissipates real heat, and its cooling fans make noise — which means it cannot share a wall with the hall you just spent Act I getting down to NC-25. Your quietest room and your noisiest closet are now negotiating.
  • The mix position. A front-of-house mix console needs to be in the room, in the audience, roughly two-thirds back and on axis. It occupies 6–10 seats you were selling. Every hall designer forgets this and every touring engineer curses them for it.
  • The load on LP-1. The AV rack is an electrical load like any other — call it 3–6 kVA for a hall your size, on its own dedicated circuit with a clean ground, never shared with dimmers. Add it to your panel schedule. That is your Week 8 work, revised.
  • The penetrations. Speaker cable, mic boxes, and conduit have to cross the acoustically-isolated envelope you detailed in Act I. Every penetration is a hole in your STC assembly.
The Trap

Gain before feedback — the microphone is in the same room as the loudspeaker.

Everything the loudspeaker emits, the microphone can hear. Turn the system up far enough and the loop goes unstable and screams. The maximum usable gain depends on the distances between talker, mic, speaker, and listener — which is why a lavalier mic (2 inches from the mouth) delivers far more usable level than a podium mic (18 inches), and why aiming the loudspeakers away from the microphones is not fussiness but physics.

The architectural consequence: where you let the designer hang the speakers, and where you put the podium, determines how loud the system can ever be. Decide it in the plan, not in the field.

§ 05

Service & Emergency Power.

Lecture 2Upstream from LP-1 · 30 min

Week 8 started at the breaker. Tonight we walk the wire back out to the street.

Your panel schedule ends at a feeder that goes somewhere. Tonight we find out where — and we find out what happens to your hall the moment that feeder dies.

The path, in order

  • Utility → transformer. Power arrives at medium voltage and is stepped down by a transformer, usually on a pad outside your building. That pad has code-mandated clearances and it is ugly. It goes in your site plan whether you like it or not.
  • Service entrance & main disconnect. Where the conductors enter the building and where the whole building can be switched off — the switch the fire department reaches for. Must be accessible.
  • Meter → main distribution panel (MDP). The utility’s cash register, then the building’s trunk.
  • Feeders → branch panels. A feeder from the MDP lands on your LP-1. That is the handshake — the point where Week 8 connected to a system it never saw.
  • Branch circuits → fixtures. Where you started. The 80% rule. Done.

The voltage decision

  • 120/208 V, 3-phase. Small commercial. Simple, and everything plugs in.
  • 277/480 V, 3-phase. The grown-up choice for a building your size. Lighting runs at 277 V — higher voltage means lower current for the same watts, which means smaller conductors and cheaper feeders. A step-down transformer then feeds the 120 V receptacles. For a hall with a big connected lighting load, this is almost certainly your answer — and it is why commercial lighting fixtures are ordered with a voltage specified.
The Room Nobody Draws

Your electrical room is architecture. It has a plan, and it is not optional.

Every panel in this course has been a table on a page. In a real building it is a steel box on a wall, in a room, that a human being has to stand in front of with the cover off and the power live.

The NEC requires working clearance in front of electrical equipment — a minimum of 3 ft 0 in of clear depth (more at higher voltages), a 30 in minimum width, and 6 ft 6 in of headroom — and that zone may not be used for storage, ductwork, piping, or your mechanical contractor's ambitions. It is dedicated space.

So: where is it in your plan? Not a rhetorical question — it is a review question, and I will ask it tonight. The electrical room needs a door, it needs to be reachable from the service entrance without crossing the house, and it should not share a wall with your NC-25 auditorium, because gear hums. If you have not drawn it, you have a hall that cannot be built.

When the power fails

Now the important half. It is Saturday night, the house is full, the show is running — and the utility feed drops. Every light you designed in Act III is on that feed. What, exactly, is still on?

The answer is defined by code, not by taste. NFPA 101 and NEC Article 700 govern the emergency system: the legally-required, life-safety-critical loads that must continue. For an assembly occupancy — which is exactly what you designed — this is not a nice-to-have. It is the difference between an evacuation and a crush.

Article 700

Emergency Systems

Legally required. Life safety. Egress illumination, exit signs, fire alarm, voice evacuation. Failure endangers people directly.

Sources: a generator, a central inverter, unit equipment (the battery pack inside each fixture — the cheap, common answer for a building your size), or a second independent utility service.

Your hall must have this.

Articles 701 / 702

Standby Systems

Legally required standby (701): loads whose failure creates hazards or hampers rescue — smoke control, some elevators, critical ventilation.

Optional standby (702): loads you decide are worth protecting — the box office server, the freezer, the data closet, the show control system. A business decision, not a code one.

Name one optional standby load in your hall and defend it.

The Cross-System Trap

A dimmer that can fail dark cannot be an egress fixture.

In Week 7 you drew four lighting layers — house, egress, work, accent — and put each on a control zone. It was a clean, elegant diagram. But the egress layer is not just another zone with a different schedule: it is a legally separate branch, fed from a different source, on its own raceway, that must come on at full output when everything else goes dark.

Which means: if your egress fixtures are on the house dimming system, a failed dimmer takes your life-safety lighting with it. Emergency fixtures either bypass the dimming entirely, or use a UL 924 emergency bypass relay that forces them to full on loss of normal power — a real product, with a real cost, that goes in your schedule.

Go look at your Week 8 panel schedule right now. If your egress circuits are mixed in with the house lighting circuits, you have found your first Encore integration conflict, and I did not even have to point at it.

How to Be Reviewed

Bring the broken thing. That is the whole point.

The strongest thing you can do in a review is open with what is wrong. It is not a confession — it is a demonstration that you can evaluate your own work, which is the single most valuable skill this course is trying to build, and it is literally what the Decision Log rubric rewards.

A polished presentation that hides a broken LPD number buys you eight comfortable minutes tonight and costs you real points on Tuesday. A student who says “my aisles are at 3 fc and I do not know why” leaves with an answer. Use the room.
