§ 03

From One Calc to a System.

Last week ended with a single defensible number: a target footcandle level and the fixture count that holds it, by hand. Hold onto it — that calc is the seed of everything tonight. The reverse form is still your workhorse:

Last Week, Still Running — The Lumen Method
E = ( N × Φ × CU × LLF ) ÷ A  →  N = ( E × A ) ÷ ( Φ × CU × LLF )
The CU still comes from the zonal-cavity method — the room cavity ratio and your surface reflectances. Tonight we keep E as the target but stop trusting the average alone: the simulation in § 06 shows what the single number hides, and the watts behind N become the load in § 07.
Four things change tonight
  • From a level to a scene. You stop lighting the room to one number and start designing states — load-in, rehearsal, show, intermission — each a stored set of layer levels.
  • From one fixture to a schedule. The single luminaire becomes a full, task-matched document covering every space.
  • From hand-calc to verified. You check the average against a false-color map and find the dark corners the average can’t see.
  • From lumens to watts. Every lumen has an electrical bill — tonight you start counting it.
The through-line Acoustics asked what the hall sounds like; daylighting, what it does with the sun; last week, how you light it at all. Tonight is the night the lighting stops being a sketch and becomes a buildable, switchable, billable system — the same leap a real project makes when the design set goes to the electrical engineer.
§ 04

Controls & Scenes.

Lecture 1Lighting the Verbs · 25 min

A hall is lit in scenes — and a scene is a set of layers at set levels.

Last week gave you the four layers. Tonight each layer gets a control: a way to turn on, dim, hold, or store. The control intent is a design decision, separate from the fixture — and it’s where most of the energy savings live.

The four control moves

  • Occupancy vs. vacancy sensing. PIR or ultrasonic sensors. Occupancy (auto-on) suits corridors and back-of-house; vacancy (manual-on, auto-off) is the energy-code default for rooms people enter deliberately — it never turns a fully daylit room on for you.
  • Daylight harvesting / dimming. The prefunction you daylit in Act II dims its electric layer as the sun does the work — a photosensor holds the target. Open-loop reads the daylight source; closed-loop reads the lit surface itself.
  • Scene presets & theatrical dimming. Stored sets of layer levels: load-in, rehearsal, pre-show, performance, intermission, cleaning — one button recalls a whole room state, no chasing faders live. The architectural layers ride a preset dimming console; the performance rig runs on its own DMX control, a separate world you leave hooks for.
  • Emergency & egress. Code-required, on a protected/illuminated circuit, comes on at power loss and stays on. UL 924 transfer devices; design for roughly 1 fc average on the egress path. Never folded into the house dimmer.
The habit to build tonight

Design the verbs, not just the fixtures.

“House dims to 10% at performance and egress holds” is a design decision you can write down and defend — independent of which downlight you picked. Capture the control intent per layer per scene; the fixture schedule says what, the controls plan says when and how bright. The two together are the lighting design.

The scene matrix — layers × states

This is the single most useful artifact in lighting controls: the four layers down the side, the room’s scenes across the top, each cell a level. Build one for your primary space and the control intent designs itself.

§ 05

The Luminaire Schedule.

Lecture 2The Document That Builds · 28 min

The schedule is what turns a sketch into a build.

A luminaire schedule is the contract: one row per fixture type, every number a fabricator or estimator needs. It is also where lumens quietly become watts — the column that feeds tonight’s electrical lecture and next week’s panel.

One row per type — the columns that matter

  • Type tag (L1, L2…) — the symbol you’ll place on the reflected ceiling plan.
  • Product & distribution — real manufacturer/model, and the distribution type (direct, diffuse, asymmetric, accent) that matches its job.
  • Delivered Φ (lm) — fixture lumens, not lamp lumens — and input W, the number the electrical side actually cares about.
  • CCT & CRI — the color decisions from last week, now committed per type.
  • Mounting & Qty — recessed / pendant / surface / track, and how many. Qty × input W is the connected load.

Every row earns its job

Distribution is a choice, not a default.

Narrow/direct over aisles and the stage apron; wide/diffuse warm light for the house and lobby; high-CRI (≥ 90) accent on the stage and displays; asymmetric wash to graze your Act I architectural surfaces. If two rows have the same distribution doing different jobs, ask whether one of them is wrong.

Watts ride along

The schedule is the load list.

Add one more column — Qty × input W — and the lighting schedule becomes the connected-load table for the whole building. That single column is what § 07 divides by area for your LPD, and what Week 8 groups into circuits and a panel schedule. Use delivered lumens for light and input watts for load — never mix them up.

§ 06

Build & Verify in LightStanza.

Lecture 3Model, Place, Calculate · 35 min

LightStanza is where the model, the fixtures, and the calc all live.

This is the heart of the night. LightStanza is our primary tool: get your geometry in, place the IES fixtures right on the plan, run the calc, and export the false-color maps and renderings. The full click-by-click is in the tutorial — here is the shape of it.

First move — get your geometry in (three ways)

  • Revit → LightStanza plugin. Place Rooms in the spaces you want analyzed, then sync — grids generate per room automatically. The preferred path if your model is in Revit.
  • Rhino drag-and-drop. Drop the .3dm / .dae straight into LightStanza; check units and materials on import.
  • No 3D model? Build it from a PDF. Import a floor-plan PDF, scale it, and draw walls and rooms with LightStanza’s own tools — the floor you draw becomes the illuminance grid. Enough to light a space without a full model.

Second move — place, calculate, read

  • Place fixtures in LightStanza. Upload each IES in the IES Manager, drop fixtures on the 2D plan, and array them at the spacing your lumen method implied (the N you solved for).
  • Run Grid Illuminance at the 30-inch workplane; read the false-color map in footcandles and export a rendering for the deliverable.
  • Check uniformity — average vs. minimum. A good average with dark corners is still a failing room.
Why this step is not optional

The average lies about the edges.

A space can hand-calc to a comfortable 25 fc average and still drop to 8 fc in the corners and under the balcony — exactly where someone trips. The lumen method literally cannot see that; it has no geometry. The simulation does. When the map and the hand-calc disagree, that gap is the finding — usually a too-optimistic CU, a distribution that throws the wrong way, or reflectances you assumed but the room doesn’t have.

§ 07

The Wire Behind the Light.

Lecture 4A Brief Bridge · 13 min

Every lumen has an electrical bill — a look ahead.

The schedule you built draws power. Tonight this is only a bridge: see where that power comes from and note the watts. The real electrical work — circuits, the panel schedule, LPD compliance, and PV — is Week 8.

Two numbers to know

  • Connected load — the sum of every fixture’s input watts (your schedule’s watts column, totaled). This is what LPD gets checked against.
  • Demand load — what the building actually draws once scenes and controls dim layers down. Lower than connected — but you size protection from connected first.

§ 08

Applied Case — The Load Side.

Back to the live Urban Colab relight from last week — the assembly / banquet auditorium that hand-calc’d to a third of its target. Last week the lumen method exposed the light deficit. Tonight, look at the other side of the same fix: buying the footcandles back cost watts. The question an energy reviewer asks next is simple — did the heavier scheme blow the LPD budget?

Two different numbers — don’t mix them. Connected load is total watts (a sum — you size wire and breakers from it). LPD is watts per square foot (a density — you check energy code against it). Same fixtures, two jobs.

* LPD = connected load ÷ ~4,411 ft² auditorium floor. Assembly allowance ≈ 0.8–1.0 W/ft² (ASHRAE 90.1 space-by-space) — the exact space-type value is confirmed in Week 8.

The finding — and the headroom

The load jumped 76% — and both schemes still clear LPD.

Reaching the IES target nearly doubled the connected load (1,016 → 1,791 W), so the LPD rose the same 76% — same floor area. Yet even the brighter scheme lands near 0.41 W/ft², comfortably under a typical assembly allowance of roughly 0.8–1.0 W/ft². Both pass; the decorative set with wide margin, the brighter LED set still with clear room.

Why the headroom? LED efficacy. Modern LED delivers so many lumens per watt that you usually have room to spare on LPD. So here the binding constraint was never the energy code — it was the footcandles. The room failed on light, not on watts.

The takeaway — but don’t ignore it

With LED, LPD is rarely the tightest limit — but it is never free.

The lesson is not “LPD doesn’t matter.” The headroom above is real but spendable: a decorative low-efficacy fixture, a much heavier layout, or a tight cavity with poor CU can eat it fast — and older sources (halogen, fluorescent) blew past these caps routinely. That is why the code exists.

So run the LPD check every time — but read it correctly. With LED you’ll usually pass, which means your design energy goes into hitting the footcandle targets and choosing efficacy, not squeaking under the cap. Total your schedule’s watts in § 10, divide by area, confirm it clears the allowance, and note it. That’s the same call the energy reviewer makes.

§ 09

Precedent — Controlled Halls.

Four reference moves for how real halls control and account for their light — each hands you something for your controls plan and your load tally.

Scene Control

The Architectural Dimming Console

Stored presets · one-button recall

House, cove, and accent layers live on a preset console (Lutron / ETC-style); an operator recalls load-in, pre-show, performance with one button instead of riding faders during a show.

Take: store scenes, don’t chase levels live — your scene matrix is the console programming.

Daylight Harvesting

The Dawn-to-Dusk Lobby

Photosensor dimming · closed loop

The prefunction you daylit holds a steady ~25 fc whether the sun or the fixtures are doing the work — a photosensor trims the electric layer continuously.

Take: your Act II daylight strategy sets the control law for the electric layer — the two acts meet at the sensor.

Life Safety

The Egress Layer Apart

UL 924 · protected circuit

Emergency and egress lighting sits on its own circuit with a transfer device, comes on at power loss, and is never dimmable with the house — roughly 1 fc on the path, always.

Take: draw egress as a separate layer and a separate circuit from the start; it’s a rubric line and a code line.

Energy Discipline

The LPD-Lean Schedule

High efficacy · controls credit

Energy-tight projects hit target light with the fewest, most efficient fixtures, then bank a controls credit (occupancy + daylight dimming) to drop further under the allowance.

Take: a short, high-lm/W schedule is the LPD win you’ll defend in Week 8 — controls are extra headroom.
