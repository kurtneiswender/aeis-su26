§ 03

From Watts to a Panel.

Last week ended with one honest number: the connected load — the sum of every fixture’s input watts on your schedule — plus three spaces lit and verified in LightStanza. Hold onto that watts column; it is the single input to everything tonight. The load doesn’t just get totaled, it gets organized: split into circuits, protected by breakers, listed on a panel, checked against the energy code, and finally offset by the sun.

That one sum is the seed of the whole night: divide it into circuits (§ 04), list them on a panel (§ 05), divide it by area for the official LPD (§ 06), and convert it to annual energy to size the PV array (§ 07). Same number, four jobs.

§ 04

Circuits & the 80% Rule.

Lecture 1Grouping the Watts · 20 min

A circuit is a group of fixtures on one breaker — and the breaker is sized with margin.

You never wire the whole schedule to one breaker. You group fixtures into branch circuits — by layer, zone, or scene — and each circuit gets a breaker you deliberately oversize, because lighting is a continuous load.

The three moves

  • Group into branch circuits. Fixtures that share a breaker. Keep a circuit to one control zone where you can — the egress layer stays on its own protected circuit, never shared with the house dimmer.
  • Size the load in VA. For LED drivers the power factor is near 1, so VA ≈ watts. Sum the connected VA on each circuit.
  • Apply the 80% rule. Lighting runs more than 3 hours — it is a continuous load, so a breaker may be loaded to only 80% of its rating. Flip it around: your circuit VA must stay under 0.8 × (breaker × volts).
The Continuous-Load Ceiling — Per Circuit
Max continuous VA = 0.80 × Breaker (A) × Volts
20 A @ 120 V
0.80 × 20 × 120 = 1,920 VA usable (2,400 VA nameplate)
20 A @ 277 V
0.80 × 20 × 277 = 4,432 VA usable — why commercial lighting loves 277 V
Rule of thumb
keep a 20 A / 120 V lighting circuit under ~1,900 VA — then start a new circuit
A single 20 A / 120 V circuit holds roughly 50–60 typical LED downlights at 32 W — but you’ll usually hit a control-zone boundary long before the electrical limit. Circuit for control first, then confirm you cleared the 80% ceiling. → Reading: MEEB Ch. 28 §28.10–28.11 (Table 28.5).
First, the power that feeds it

Single-phase vs. three-phase — and why lighting likes 277 V.

Single-phase power is one alternating push–pull — the 120/240 V service in a house. Two hot legs; fine for small buildings. Three-phase power is three alternating voltages staggered a third of a cycle (120°) apart — legs A, B, C. The overlapping pushes deliver steadier power, carry more load on smaller wire, and are what motors and big HVAC need — so nearly every commercial and institutional building, your auditorium included, takes a three-phase service.

Three-phase hands you a voltage ladder: measure a hot leg to neutral and you get the lower line-to-neutral voltage; measure between two hot legs and you get the higher line-to-line voltage. Two ladders are standard — see below.

Ladder 1 · mid-size buildings

120 / 208 V, 3-phase, 4-wire

120 V leg-to-neutral runs receptacles and small/residential-scale lighting; 208 V leg-to-leg feeds larger single- and three-phase loads. Lighting on the same 120 V as the outlets — simple, common in smaller projects.

Ladder 2 · commercial standard

277 / 480 V, 3-phase, 4-wire

277 V leg-to-neutral is the commercial lighting voltage; 480 V leg-to-leg feeds big motors and chillers. Receptacles get 120 V through a step-down transformer. Assume this for the auditorium and put lighting panel LP-1 at 277 V.

Why 277 V wins for lighting

Same breaker, 2.3× the light.

A 20 A circuit’s continuous ceiling is 0.8 × amps × volts. At 120 V that is 1,920 VA; at 277 V it is 4,432 VA — the same breaker and wire carry 2.3× the connected wattage. So a 277 V lighting design needs fewer circuits, less copper, and a smaller panel. That is why commercial lighting is almost always 277 V — and why we run LP-1 there.

The habit to build tonight

Circuit by zone, verify by math.

Draw circuit groups that match your control intent from Week 7 — house on its own, egress on a protected circuit, accent/cove grouped, back-of-house separate. Then total each group’s VA and confirm it clears the 80% ceiling. If a zone exceeds ~1,900 VA at 120 V, split it into two circuits — that’s a design decision, not a failure.

§ 05

The Panel Schedule.

Lecture 2The Document Engineers Stamp · 20 min

The panel schedule is the ledger of the building’s power.

One row per circuit: what it feeds, its connected VA, and its breaker. Total it and you have the panel’s connected load — the number that sizes the feeder and the service. This is the electrical twin of your luminaire schedule.

The columns that matter

  • Circuit # — the position in the panel (odd on the left, even on the right — that’s just how panels are drawn).
  • Description — what it serves: “House downlights — north half,” “Egress / aisle,” “Cove & accent.”
  • Connected VA — the summed load on that circuit (from your luminaire schedule).
  • Breaker — the trip size (20 A typical for lighting), chosen so VA clears the 80% ceiling.

One number, two schedules

The luminaire schedule feeds the panel.

Every VA in the panel traces back to a Qty × input W in last week’s luminaire schedule. If they don’t reconcile, one of them is wrong. Build the panel by grouping luminaire-schedule rows into circuits — don’t invent new loads.

Balance & spares

Spread the load, leave room.

Keep the two legs of the panel roughly balanced, keep egress on its own protected circuit, and leave a couple of spare breakers — every real panel does. The total connected VA is what sizes the feeder back to the main panel from Week 7’s power diagram.

§ 06

LPD, Made Official.

Lecture 3The Energy-Code Verdict · 18 min

Last week’s first pass becomes a real pass or fail.

You already know LPD = connected W ÷ area. Tonight you stop estimating the allowance and use the actual ASHRAE 90.1 (or IECC) space-by-space value for each of your room types — and, if your controls earn it, take the credit.

The move — space-by-space

  • Match each space to its allowance. Auditorium/assembly, lobby, corridor, restroom, and storage each have their own W/ft² cap — you don’t get one building number.
  • Check installed vs. allowed, per space. Installed = that space’s connected W ÷ its area. Pass if installed ≤ allowed.
  • Claim the controls credit if earned. Occupancy/vacancy sensing and daylight-responsive dimming — the Week 7 controls plan — can add allowance back. If you designed the control, you may get the credit.

* Illustrative ranges — confirm the exact space-type value in the ASHRAE 90.1 / IECC table for your edition. The point tonight is to use a real space-type number, not a single guessed building average.  → Reading: MEEB Ch. 28, Table 28.1 (lighting VA/ft²) & §28.5 (ASHRAE 90.1).

The finding to expect

With LED you’ll usually pass — but the tight rooms are where it’s real.

The house almost always clears (that’s Week 7’s lesson — the binding constraint was footcandles, not watts). The interesting checks are the tight caps: a corridor or storage room with a decorative fixture or a heavy layout can actually fail. Run every space, and where a space is tight, that’s where the controls credit or a higher-efficacy fixture earns its keep. Report each space’s installed vs. allowed — one line each.

§ 07

Sizing the PV Array.

Lecture 4Offsetting the Load · 20 min

The last move: put the sun back on the ledger.

You know the connected watts and how the scenes run them. Turn that into annual energy, size an array to offset a target share of it, then weigh what it costs against the electricity it buys back. On a big flat auditorium roof, area is rarely the binding constraint — the honest question is the money.

The four moves

  • Annual lighting energy. Connected kW × equivalent full-load hours per year. Controls and scenes mean the lights rarely run at 100% — use a realistic operating estimate, not 8,760 h.
  • Detroit’s solar resource. Roughly 4.2 peak sun-hours/day — about 1,250 kWh per kW of array per year after losses.
  • Size for a target offset. Array kW = (annual kWh × offset %) ÷ specific yield. (A rooftop needs ~67 ft²/kW — worth a glance, but rarely the limit here.)
  • Cost it, and find the payback. Installed cost ≈ $/W × array watts; annual savings = offset kWh × utility rate; simple payback = cost ÷ savings.
From Array to Payback
Array kW = ( E × offset ) ÷ Yield
Cost = kW × 1000 × $/W  ·  Payback = Cost ÷ ( E × offset × rate )

annual lighting energy, kWh/yr = connected kW × operating hours

This is the block to unstick your model — we’ll work the common problems live on real files, then take open questions on anything.

Come prepared — so we can actually fix it

Bring the broken thing, not a description of it.

  • Have the file open — the LightStanza project, Revit model, or Rhino file that’s misbehaving, ready to screen-share.
  • Know the exact symptom — the error text, the wrong number, or the step where it stalls. “It doesn’t work” is hard to fix in a room of fifteen.
  • Confirm your LightStanza access first. If you still can’t log in, say so at the top of the hour — account setup is the one thing that blocks everything else. (See the account-access note the class already received.)
  • Post the snag on the whiteboard as you hit it during the work session, so nothing gets lost.
